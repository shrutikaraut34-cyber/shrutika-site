There's a feeling that is very much unexplainable for most people. You don't feel happy or even okay. And it's not depression either. There's something underneath the still waters of your mind that you just can't see. Every day goes by like the wind, but you can't quite grasp the details of what's happening. You're able to function, but there's a fogginess to everything. The world feels like it has lost some of its vibrancy. But you don't feel sad per se, you just feel… meh. There is a word for this feeling - Languish.

Most of us have felt languish. For some, it's difficult to keep track of the days. Even though major events happen around them, they can't seem to feel like a part of it. For others, they feel like a mere viewer of their own life. Like a kite flying above just watching everything happen with a bird's-eye view. It's not because they don't care, but they feel unable to. A simple question of "how are you?" becomes a deep internal philosophical debate.

In psychology, languish is the void between wellness and illness. It is the opposite of flourish. To languish is the inability to experience the enthusiasm or joy of life, feeling stuck in the same place and feeling detached from the day-to-day. Hobbies start to lose their appeal, daily tasks seem harder to complete, the body feels fatigued, and there is an overall lack of purpose in life. It seems like a crisis, but just not bad enough to warrant a strong reaction. So you're stuck feeling confused and emotionally exhausted.

## Is there science behind it?

Now for the question - is this just a theory, or is there actual science behind this? The problem is that we are taught to view psychology as black and white, healthy and unhealthy. In actuality, there is a lot of unexplored space in between these states. Mental health is not just the absence of mental "illness". Being free of a diagnosis doesn't mean you're mentally healthy. You can be "not ill" and still not be mentally well. This grey area is where languish sits; which is why it's so difficult to pin down. The wrong assumption is that just because someone doesn't have depression, anxiety or other issues is the reason for this gap. As Adam Grant described it in his essay on the "blah" so many of us feel, it's the "neglected middle child" of mental health.

A 2002 research by Corey Keyes, the person who coined the term, shows that individuals who claim to be flourishing are surprisingly rare - only about 1 in 5 adults said they were truly flourishing. Most people (roughly 65%) are simply coasting in "moderate" mental health, and around 1 in 6 is languishing. Two decades after Keyes showed that most people aren't flourishing, the picture has only darkened for the young. The 2025 Global Flourishing Study of around 200,000 people found that 18 to 29 year olds now flourish the least of any age group - which explains the reason languish seems to feel like a generational condition.

One of the largest wellbeing studies ever conducted backs this up. Harvard and Gallup researchers found that young people are now flourishing less than their parents and grandparents - almost a reversal of what psychologists long assumed about age and happiness. It is also a reflection of the post-capitalist state of the world, where opportunities seem to be shrinking, and people feel like life has lost meaning. Along with this, the domino effect of one global event after another has given rise to a popular sentiment: "We're living in the end times".

Each of these reasons deserves its own dissection, but the result is the same. A large number of people are not able to get out of this state of detachment. A 2024 study of young adults showed that many felt like they were "stuck in a deep hole" just looking as their life milestones - friendships, first jobs, momentum - quietly slipped away, while they waited for life to return to normal. The good thing is - once you can identify the feeling, you can start to work on it.

## How to work through it

**Name it.** Research on emotion differentiation - the skill of labeling your emotions precisely rather than as a vague "bad", links it to better coping and mental health (Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight, 2015). Giving the feeling a name shifts you from "what's wrong with me?" to "what do I actually need?"

**Act before you feel like it (behavioral activation).** One of the most effective clinical tools for low, flat mood is behavioral activation, which is deliberately scheduling small, meaningful, or rewarding activities before the motivation to do them returns, rather than waiting to feel better first. It's an evidence-based treatment for depression, and it echoes that 2024 study of young adults, where those who practiced "intentional action" got meaningfully better results.

**Rebuild engagement through flow.** The scattered "meh" of languishing is, in many ways, the opposite of flow, which is the state of deep involvement in a challenging task. This is described as one of the most reliable sources of engagement and meaning. For this, section out uninterrupted blocks of time for this flow state, with your phone on silent and your undivided attention towards meditation or peaceful or interesting tasks.

**Feed your basic psychological needs.** Self-Determination Theory identifies three needs that are parts of overall wellbeing: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Languishing often signals that one of these parts is lacking. So reclaim a small choice that's yours (autonomy), finish something you can point to (competence), and reach for people who make you feel connected (relatedness).

**Reconnect with meaning and mattering.** If you can once again feel like your life has meaning and that you matter in the grand scheme, you can shift your mindset from empty to engaged. Small acts of contribution like volunteering, engaging with a good cause, donating to those who need it, being useful to another person, etc. can all help in building up one's sense of community and purpose.

These practices are small ways to help you feel better, but they can't replace actual human input. Sometimes it really helps to get an outsider's point of view on why you feel unable to connect with your day to day life. If the emptiness keeps on increasing, it is more likely to result in anxiety, depression or other mental issues. Whenever you can, talk to a friend or a loved one. Getting the input of people close to you helps clear up your own thoughts and emotions. Counseling and therapy are also a great way to align any mental jumble. To reiterate, lack of mental illness doesn't result in mental health. Even if there's a lack of diagnosis, it can be helpful to get checked up every once in a while.
