In modern times, dating and romance has been poked and prodded endlessly, to the point where we have more contradicting information than actually helpful advice. The conversation is more focused on "if they wanted to they would", "doing the bare minimum", "what you bring to the table", and more standards placed on companionship. And above all, the label to avoid the most: being clingy.

Being labeled as a clingy person has become one of the most feared categorizations in dating. People desperately want to present as aloof or "above it", where caring too much has become a weakness. Desperation is seen as pathetic and weak, and not caring seems to be the ideal. A critical analysis of online dating found that the endless menu of profiles pushes us into a "shopping" mindset, where people become options to be filtered rather than humans to be known. This is one of the reasons why dating burnout is being observed more and more among the population - it has become more about positioning yourself as cool instead of being genuine. Thus emerges the paradox of more dating but less connections, and asking for affection becomes neediness.

## What "clingy" has come to mean

Since the word clingy has been taken over, its meaning has become more encompassing. In the past, clingy used to mean a constant need for validation, assurance and connection. It used to signify someone who is fearful of rejection and thus takes it out on the other person, by being overly pushy of personal boundaries, and panicking when there is no immediate response.

But now, clingy has come to mean wanting a text back, wanting to make plans, wanting to know if this is going somewhere and other basic relationship elements. The desire to have a deeper bond has been labeled as too much. People who fear intimacy feel like it's easier to say "you're being clingy" instead of communicating. And sometimes just a mirror of the other person's discomfort with being wanted.

This is reflected in the fact that even though many single people use dating apps, there is an observable dating recession that is taking over the youth. People want to connect, but feel like they can't. This is partially due to the hyper independent culture that has made wanting intimacy into something to be embarrassed about. When the focus goes more into individualism, wanting connection becomes viewed as clinginess. And this has contributed to the rise of "ghosting", "situationships", "breadcrumbing" and more. The person who cares more seems to be the one who "loses".

## What attachment theory says

In recent times, wanting love has been labeled as weakness. But is it? One proposed theory about attachment talks about how attachment formed during adulthood can be the same as attachment formed during childhood. According to research by Hazan C and Shaver P. (1987), adult attachment style distributions mirror those of infancy, categorized into secure (\~56%) characterized by trust and lasting relationships, avoidant (\~25%) marked by a fear of intimacy, and anxious/ambivalent (\~19%) defined by obsessive worry and fear of abandonment. Thus we are wired to seek closeness, just as children do with parents.

Beneath all of these human connection patterns is attachment theory, and it explains the two opposite ways people handle the fear of being vulnerable. Researchers Mario Mikulincer and Phillip Shaver describe them as two insecure strategies.

- **Anxious attachment**, where lack of attachment becomes an alarm. Distance causes the person to increase their demand for closeness: anxiety shown to any sign of rejection, need for constant reassurance and more. It's usually learned from lack of steady affection in childhood.
- **Avoidant attachment**, where a request for attachment becomes an alarm. These people value independence to the extreme, feeling crowded by closeness, and treat needing anyone as a weakness. This isn't the absence of needs; it's an attachment system that stems from their emotional needs being dismissed in childhood, teaching them that depending on others is pointless.

Often, people with anxious and avoidant attachment end up together, where the cycle of push and pull. This is often where the "clingy" label stems from. And the mere suggestion of being labeled clingy makes some people swing the complete opposite way - i.e., ghosting, breadcrumbing and other forms of emotional distancing. People would rather lose a connection than lose their idea of independence.

## The dependency paradox

However, when we start to treat independence and love like a tug-of-war, we start forming the belief that leaning on someone must cost you your freedom. Research shows the opposite. In a series of studies known as the dependency paradox, psychologist Brooke Feeney found that people who felt they could depend on a responsive partner became more independent. They took on harder challenges, explored more, and were likelier to reach their own goals. Therapist Sue Johnson, who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, calls this "effective dependency". Having a safe person to rely on is exactly what gives us the courage to go out into the world. In terms of childhood attachment carrying onto adulthood, we feel more comfortable "exploring the playground". The reliance on another person is something we don't outgrow.

To bring it back to modern dating - if you ever feel uncomfortable when asking for affection, know that wanting love isn't a weakness. In the same way, if you find yourself unable to accept affection, know that receiving love doesn't make you weak. This isn't just encouragement, it's based on empirical evidence. Try to step out of the assumptions and stereotypes and view love and relationships in an objective way. Connection is very much human, and it requires vulnerability, and sometimes a little clinginess.
