There always seems to be one thing left to say. One thing to clear up. One blame to resolve. One hurt to understand. One last conversation that will just explain everything and make you feel better. But the more you think about it, the more things seem to need explanation or justification. It never ends at just one thing. And so the feeling of unfinished business never quite goes away. We as humans always crave "closure".

Closure can mean a variety of things for different people. The American Psychological Association (APA) defines closure as the sense of reaching a resolution, whether it be for a past trauma, the end of a relationship, a psychological issue, or something that has been bothering you repeatedly. The core idea is that if you can just understand why they did that, why you did that, or why that happened, then everything will make sense and you can finally move on. However, as anyone who has tried to get closure can attest, this very rarely happens. There will always be new opinions, new perspectives, and new questions added that make it impossible to reach a verdict that feels fully satisfactory.

## Why some minds can't sit with an open question

From a psychological standpoint, needing closure is a measurable trait, and it has a cognitive explanation attached to it. Research shows that for some people, any answer is better than no answer. It describes how some minds are wired to desire predictability, with a preference for order and structure, and an intense discomfort with ambiguity. This was measured through a questionnaire called the "Need for Closure Scale." It showed that some minds can't sit with an open question - they grab the nearest answer to make the discomfort stop, then hold on tight to the conclusion. A high need for closure isn't just being anxious, bossy or obsessive. It is a distinct trait of its own. This is a major reason why friendship, relationship or familial breakups cause significant distress and a craving for closure, or "one last conversation".

There are two theories that complement these explanations for the need for closure in humans: the Zeigarnik Effect and the Ovsiankina Effect. The Zeigarnik Effect is the psychological phenomenon where incomplete tasks are more memorable than completed ones. Basically, whenever you feel like there is unfinished business or a lack of closure on a situation, your brain tends to remember it in more detail. The natural continuation of this is the Ovsiankina Effect, where your brain feels an intense need to complete the unfinished task, resulting in behaviour that drives people to re-engage with things they've left open. That can look like repeatedly contacting someone even when they don't want to talk, all in the hope of getting some form of closure.

## Why it hurts so much

Two fMRI studies help explain why this hurts so much. One found that romantic rejection lights up the brain's reward, craving and addiction systems - the same circuitry involved in drug withdrawal. The other, comparing social and physical pain tasks, showed that social rejection runs on the same brain circuit we use to feel physical pain. Our social and emotional responses are so strong that an intense rejection, or the sudden end of a relationship, gets registered by the brain as genuine physical pain.

Focusing on the romantic aspect, relationships can be deeply intertwined with our sense of self as well as our daily lives, which is why the end of a relationship causes observable changes in individuals. Having a romantic partner means a merging of daily activities, social networks, goals, and even aspects of our self-concept. So when such a crucial relationship ends, closure can seem like the only way to rebuild the part of your self-identity that is lost. The same can be said for any relationship that plays a huge part in one's life.

## Writing your own ending

Closure seems like the only option for resolution because, for many people, it quietly becomes a compulsion. The hard part about closure is that it rarely comes from the other person, and waiting around for them to give it to you just keeps the open loop running in your head. So the more useful goal is to build your own comfort with not knowing. This is exactly what approaches like Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) are built around - learning to sit with the discomfort of an unanswered question instead of scrambling to shut it down.

One of the simplest ways to start is to give yourself the conversation you never got. Writing an unsent letter, where you pour out everything you wish you could say and then never send it, has been shown to help people process painful experiences and make sense of them on their own terms. You don't actually need their version of the ending. You're allowed to write your own. It also helps to remember that letting go isn't one clean decision - it's a slow shift in how you feel.

Research on forgiveness shows that simply deciding to be over someone doesn't really do much; it's the emotional letting go, when the anger and the hurt finally soften, that loosens the memory's grip. Sometimes the most freeing thing is to accept that some endings just never close all the way. Grief researchers even argue that closure itself is a bit of a myth - that the point isn't to tie a neat bow on it, but to learn to carry it and keep living anyway.

So the next time your mind is screaming for an answer, try to catch that urge to grab the first explanation you can find, and just sit with the not-knowing a little longer. Talk it out with someone you trust - even a counselor - and let time do its quiet work. You can be okay, even without ever getting the full story, or giving one of your own.
