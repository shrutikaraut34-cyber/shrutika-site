When was the last time you cried? Some answer with "Oh, I watched this really sad war documentary", or "I saw this video of a dog crying and I couldn't stop crying", or even "I was listening to Lana Del Ray yesterday, and I cried like a baby". But when you ask them if they remember crying over something sad in their own real life, they might say, "Hmm… It's been months; I can't even remember". In some cases it's even years. Seems bizarre… and then the question arises - how come that watching YouTube videos made you cry, but you can't remember crying about something real that happened to you?

## Grief through the ages

For this, we have to go back to the age-old question of why we put ourselves through manufactured grief. Throughout history, there have been poems, songs, plays, myths, stories, and more about death, lament, unrequited yearning and losing the one you love. From Sanskrit to Greek, Sumerian to Latin, all of the oldest civilizations have put pen to paper (or chisel to stone) and composed beautiful words about the pain of being human and experiencing life and its many complications. It's a form of emotional release practiced for millennia, both by the creators and the patrons of art.

In Sanskrit, the Natya Shastra, which is a comprehensive deep dive into the performing arts, has divided all forms of art into Rasa (aesthetic or essence). One of them is the Karuna Rasa, or the essence of sadness, compassion or despair. In Ancient Greece, comedy and tragedy emerged as the two main genres of drama and theatre. Tragedy took on the form of mournful stories depicting the worst of human suffering, with the goal of collective venting. Audiences wept together for the characters.

One way or another, grief finds a way out. It can be beautifully tragic and greatly moving. It torments us, it makes us sad, it makes us cry. The Passion of the Christ, Romeo and Juliet, Oedipus Rex, Muna Madan, Sishir Basanta… the list is endless. But why do we still indulge?

The simple answer is because it makes us feel deep emotions. And why does it even matter? Aren't sad things supposed to make us cry anyways? But again - why do we seek out these tormenting emotions through art? Why do we want to make ourselves cry in this way? Why would we choose to hurt our own feelings? In addition, the cognitive dissonance of crying over stories but not being able to cry as easily in painful real-life situations displays a real dichotomy in the human brain.

## Why art feels safe to cry to

This tendency forces us to question human nature itself. In simple words, we feel safer letting our emotions out through art because it feels safer and more removed from our own life. It is an easier way to process our emotions without actually having to face tragedy. It is a quantifiable phenomenon, which is why the desire to feel sadness through the safety of consuming art has been looked into through various philosophies and theories.

Aristotle's answer to this was Katharsis, which was carried into English as Catharsis. This is the emotional pouring out of heavy feelings like sadness, despair, pain, or loss experienced by audiences in tragic drama. Theatre audiences used to cry collectively while witnessing theatre tragedies. For Abhinavagupta (Kashmir, 10th-11th century CE), experiencing sorrow through art was about savoring a suffering that is no longer personally yours. The pain and grief are lifted out of their real-life stakes, depersonalized into the universal essence of sorrow, which you then experience through depersonalization (disconnect from your own self and being a mere observer).

A more modern look into this phenomenon was done by Pleasurable Compassion Theory (Huron & Vuoskoski, 2020). This theory talks about how the consumption and enjoyment of art that saddens us runs through compassion and empathy. While listeners may experience unpleasant emotions, they also experience strong empathic concern (compassion). This feeling is inherently pleasurable due to its pro-social elements, and is strongest in people high in trait empathy.

Another influential idea - the Distancing-Embracing model (Menninghaus et al., 2017) - focuses on why art in particular feels safe enough to fall apart to. It suggests that art moves us through two things happening at the same time. The first is distancing: the built-in awareness that what we are watching isn't real, that we are safe, with no actual threat or consequence attached to it. The second is embracing: once that safety is in place, we are free to let the emotion all the way in, and even find it beautiful and moving. In other words, the very fact that a story isn't real is what gives us permission to feel it completely.

## Why real grief is harder

This is also why psychologists treat crying at a film or a song as a genuine form of emotional processing, and not some lesser or borrowed version of feeling. The story becomes a safe container for emotions that might be too big or too tangled to face directly. Real life rarely offers that same safety - real problems carry stakes, consequences, and things that can actually go wrong, so we tend to hold ourselves together instead. And when something is truly painful, the mind often does the opposite of processing it. Under real threat, loss, or trauma, the nervous system can numb and wall the feelings off simply to keep us functioning (SAMHSA, 2014). We slip into survival mode, and the tears get locked somewhere we can't quite reach.

You cannot be happy if you have never experienced sadness. One needs the other, like yin and yang. And experiencing pain through art is a lot easier than facing your own demons and experiencing personal grief. So, crying to a song but not crying when something deeply hurts you is a deeply embedded defense mechanism. However you do it, processing your emotions is a core part of having good mental health. So the next time you feel emotionally constipated, grab some tissues, crank up the emo music, put on the sad movie, and cry your heart out. That's the way it's been done since art was first created.
